First Principles in Traffic Video Analytics: A Deterministic Engineering Approach to Roadway Monitoring

By Dipak Kumar • 10 min read • October 2026
"Look at the thing itself. What is it in its own nature? Strip it bare of its external reputation, its name, and the words used to describe it, and see what it actually does." — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations

The Engineering Objective

Before a highway bridge is widened, an industrial bypass is funded, or an intersection is timed, transportation engineers must answer four concrete questions: How many vehicles passed this cross-section of road, in which direction, at what exact time, and with what headway?

This article outlines a direct, 6-step deterministic methodology to derive those answers from standard roadside CCTV/DVR footage on an office laptop (<12W CPU) with 100% human-auditable visual proof.

1. The Physical Signal: Ground Contact & Optical Mass

If we strip away computer vision jargon, what is a vehicle crossing a roadside camera’s field of view? It is not an abstract probability vector. It is a physical optical mass with positive ground contact moving across a bounded pavement plane over time.

From this first principle, three physical constraints govern the entire pipeline:

  • The Road Has Bounded Geometry: Perspective distortion dictates that distant objects occupy fewer vertical pixels than identical objects nearby.
  • The Asphalt is Static, Traffic is Dynamic: Pavement color remains stable over minutes, while vehicles arrive, occlude, and depart.
  • Motion is Directional and Chronological: A vehicle traveling eastbound must break line A before line B. Oscillating shadows swing across both lines simultaneously or erratically.

2. Step 1: Perspective-Invariant Optical Scale $H(x)$

Standard cameras view highways under oblique perspective angles. A compact sedan near the camera may appear 180 pixels tall, while a large freight truck in the background appears only 65 pixels tall. Fixed pixel area thresholds inevitably fail across varying distances.

$$H(x) = y_{\text{near}}(x) - y_{\text{far}}(x)$$
Local Optical Road Scale

The quantity $H(x)$ defines the local optical scale of the roadway. Every spatial threshold in the pipeline—from minimum blob area and tracking centroid tolerance to morphological kernel dimensions—is defined as a scale-normalized fraction of $H(x)$:

$$\text{Tolerance}_{\text{normalized}}(x) = \gamma \cdot H(x)$$
Scale-Normalized Dimensionless Threshold

Much as railroad rails provide an intrinsic geometric ruler along a track regardless of perspective depth, $H(x)$ serves as the self-calibrating physical baseline. A distant truck and a foreground sedan are evaluated on an identical, scale-invariant footing.

3. Step 2: The Ground Reference: Reconstructing Clean Asphalt

To detect vehicles without training neural networks, the system must know what the empty road looks like. On an active corridor, moving traffic rarely leaves the pavement vacant. We reconstruct the pristine asphalt baseline directly from moving traffic using the temporal median:

$$\mathcal{B}(x, y) = \text{median}_{t \in \mathcal{S}} \Big\{ \mathcal{I}(x, y, t) \Big\}$$
Temporal Median Asphalt Operator

By calculating the median pixel intensity across 60 to 120 uniformly spaced sample frames, transient foreground vehicles dissolve mathematically. Moving vehicles occupy any given pixel for only a small fraction of the window, allowing the median to isolate unmoving asphalt without ghosting artifacts.

4. Step 3: Eliminating False Triggers: The Chronological Two-Line Gate

A single virtual tripwire across a road triggers on swaying utility poles, passing clouds, and headlight reflections. To eliminate environmental noise deterministically, we enforce a Chronological Dual-Line Verification Protocol:

$$\text{Pass}_{\text{valid}} \iff \exists \, (t_1 < t_2) : \quad x(t_1) = x_{\text{count}} \;\;\land\;\; x(t_2) = x_{\text{verify}}$$
Dual-Line Crossing Condition

A valid passage requires an optical mass to break the Counting Line at $t_1$, and subsequently break the Verification Line at $t_2$. Wind-swayed shadows and illumination ripples fail this test because they oscillate across boundaries or strike both lines simultaneously; only an actual body with forward momentum fulfills $t_1 < t_2$. The signed displacement $\Delta x = x(t_2) - x(t_1)$ simultaneously resolves travel direction (LR vs RL).

5. Step 4: Solving the "Ghost Semi-Trailer": In-Frame Articulated Coupling

When a tractor pulls a 53-foot semi-trailer, the physical gap above the fifth wheel reveals asphalt behind the cab. Standard detectors register the cab and trailer as two separate vehicles traveling at identical speeds with an impossible headway of $0.35\,\text{s}$.

$$\Delta x_{\text{gap}} \le 0.45 \cdot H(x) \quad \text{and} \quad \Delta y_{\text{contact}} \le 0.15 \cdot H(x)$$
In-Frame Coupling Criteria

Before a record is logged, the tracker inspects adjacent blobs: do both components share the same pavement ground line ($y_{\text{bottom}}$), and is their in-frame gap within physical hitch clearances? If both hold, the tractor and trailer are welded into a single commercial vehicle, eliminating double-counts at the source.

6. Step 5: Day-to-Night Continuity: Morphological Headlight Pairing

At night, a vehicle ceases to be a single solid silhouette and splits into two isolated beams of light. Under standard daytime morphology, these appear as two small distinct objects.

The system continuously monitors the median asphalt luma. When illumination drops below the twilight threshold ($\tau_{\text{night}} \approx 75$), the processing kernel switches adaptively from a compact daytime box ($0.18 \times 0.13\,H(x)$) to a horizontally elongated nocturnal ellipse ($0.49 \times 0.18\,H(x)$). This wide horizontal kernel physically bridges the two headlight beams across the carriageway, maintaining continuous vehicle detection through midnight.

Epilogue: Restraint as an Engineering Principle

In engineering, true elegance is found when the methodology matches the simplicity of the physical problem.

A camera is a sensor of light. A road is a geometric plane. A vehicle is an optical mass traveling in time. When software respects those physical realities, counting traffic becomes a straightforward, reliable task of geometry, clean mathematics, and the disciplined judgment of a human in the loop.
